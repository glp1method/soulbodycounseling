Psychedelic-Assisted Therapy: What's Real, What's Hype, and Where I Stand

Ketamine clinics are in strip malls. Psilocybin is legal in licensed Colorado healing centers and heading toward FDA review. Here's what the evidence actually supports, and what it doesn't.

Zane Guilfoyle, LPC, LAC November 2026 11 min read

More clients ask me about psychedelics now than ask me about antidepressants. Usually it comes up near the end of a session, a little sideways: So I've been reading about psilocybin. Or: A friend did ketamine and said it changed his life. Is that real?

It's a fair question, and it deserves a better answer than the two you usually get. One is the breathless version, where a single mushroom ceremony undoes thirty years of depression. The other is the reflexive "drugs are bad" version, which ignores some of the most interesting clinical research of the last two decades.

The honest answer sits somewhere less exciting and more useful. Some of this is real. Some of it is being sold faster than the science can support. And almost none of it works the way the marketing suggests.

Where things actually stand right now

It helps to separate the substances, because they're at very different points.

Ketamine is the only one you can get legally from a prescriber today. A nasal form, esketamine (Spravato), was FDA-approved in 2019 for treatment-resistant depression, and it's given under supervision in a clinic. Most of the IV ketamine clinics you see advertised are using it off-label. It can work fast, sometimes within hours, which is genuinely remarkable for people who've been stuck for years. The catch is that the effect often fades, so many people end up in ongoing maintenance sessions.

Psilocybin is the one closest to the finish line. Compass Pathways has reported positive Phase 3 results for its psilocybin treatment for treatment-resistant depression and expects to finish its FDA application by the end of this year. If it's approved and the DEA reschedules it, the company is targeting 2027. That is not the same as available, and it's not guaranteed.

Colorado got there first in its own way. Under the Natural Medicine Health Act that voters passed in 2022, adults 21 and over can legally use psilocybin at state-licensed healing centers with a trained facilitator, and some of those centers are now open. Personal use is decriminalized too, though selling it outside the licensed system isn't legal.

MDMA looked like it would be first. In 2024, the FDA declined to approve it for PTSD and asked for another Phase 3 trial, citing concerns about how the studies were run and how the data held up. That was a real setback, and the reasons for it matter (more on that below).

Ibogaine is getting attention as a possible treatment for opioid addiction, and Texas has committed $50 million to clinical trials after it couldn't find a drug company to fund them. Colorado's advisory board has recommended adding it to the state program, but it isn't available here yet. It also carries serious cardiac risks, and people have died from it in unregulated settings abroad. Promising and dangerous at the same time.

What the evidence actually shows

The research is real. Across multiple trials, psilocybin paired with therapy has produced meaningful drops in depression for a lot of people who hadn't responded to anything else. Some of them got there after one or two sessions instead of years of daily medication. That's not nothing. If you've watched someone you love try four SSRIs, you understand why people are excited.

But the headlines leave out some things.

The blinding problem. Good drug trials work because nobody knows who got the real thing. With psychedelics, almost everyone knows within an hour. That makes it much harder to separate the drug's effect from the effect of expecting a breakthrough, especially among people who signed up specifically hoping for one.

It isn't always better than what we already have. In a well-known 2021 trial in the New England Journal of Medicine, psilocybin was compared head to head with a standard antidepressant (escitalopram). On the main measure the researchers chose in advance, there wasn't a statistically significant difference. Psilocybin did better on some secondary measures, and it worked faster, but "better than placebo" and "better than the pill you can already get" are different claims.

The therapy is doing more of the work than the ads admit. In the research, nobody just takes a pill. There's preparation beforehand, trained people in the room during, and follow-up sessions after. When the MDMA trials ran into trouble with the FDA, part of the problem was that it was hard to say what the therapy component even was, or how consistently it was delivered. You can't approve a therapy you can't define.

Durability is still an open question. Some people stay better for months or longer. Some don't. Early data suggests effects can fade, and we don't yet have good long-term answers about who keeps the benefit and why.

What's being oversold

The biggest piece of hype is the one-and-done story. A single big experience can absolutely crack something open. Old grief, a sudden sense of being loved, a clear view of a pattern you couldn't see from inside it. Those moments are real, and I don't dismiss them.

But an insight isn't the same as a change. I've talked to plenty of people who had a profound experience and were back in exactly the same life six weeks later, now with the added weight of feeling like they wasted a miracle. The insight showed them the door. Walking through it still takes the slow, unglamorous work of doing things differently on a Tuesday.

The second piece of hype is that a nice room and a calm facilitator make it safe for everyone. They help a lot. They don't change your medical history. In Colorado's system, facilitators complete state-approved training, but they aren't required to be clinicians, and the screening isn't the same as a psychiatric evaluation. That matters more for some people than others.

Who should slow down

This isn't medical advice, and if you're seriously considering any of this, talk to a prescriber who knows your history. But here are the things I'd want anyone to know going in:

A personal or family history of psychosis or bipolar disorder is the biggest red flag. Psychedelics can trigger episodes in people who are vulnerable, and those people were mostly excluded from the trials, so we know less about them, not more.

Medications matter. Lithium combined with psychedelics has been linked to seizures. Antidepressants can blunt the effect, and stopping them abruptly to "make room" for a psilocybin session can cause its own problems. Don't change your medications without your prescriber.

Your heart matters, especially with ibogaine, and to a lesser degree with MDMA and ketamine, which raise blood pressure and heart rate.

Your state of mind matters, too. If you're in acute crisis, or if your life is unstable in ways that would make a hard experience harder to land, it's worth waiting. A big experience isn't a substitute for having ground to stand on afterward.

The part nobody in the wellness ads talks about

For a lot of gay men, psychedelics aren't new. Ketamine has been part of the party scene for decades. "K" at a club or a chemsex weekend is the same molecule people are now getting in a clinic, and some of the men I work with have a complicated history with it.

Context changes everything. The same substance used to stay up, numb out, or get through sex you're not fully present for can do something very different in a setting built for safety, intention, and support. That's not a moral distinction. It's the same idea I wrote about in the BDSM post: the container shapes what happens inside it.

It also means it's worth being honest with yourself about why you're interested. Wanting to heal and wanting to escape can feel identical from the inside. If substance use has been a problem for you before, that doesn't automatically rule anything out, but it does mean you deserve a real conversation first, not a sales call. (If that's where you are, my post on harm reduction is a decent place to start.)

Where I stand

I don't provide psychedelics, and I'm not going to tell anyone where to get them. What I can do is help on either side of it: thinking clearly about whether it makes sense for you, what you're hoping it'll do, and what the risks look like for your particular history. And afterward, making sense of what came up, so it turns into something you live rather than something you just remember.

That second part is where I think most of the healing actually happens. A lot of what surfaces in these experiences is the same material I work with every day: old shame, grief that never got space, a nervous system that's been braced for so long it forgot what rest feels like. The experience can bring it all to the surface. Then it still needs somewhere to go.

I'm genuinely interested in this research. I think some of these medicines will turn out to be important tools, especially for people that conventional treatment hasn't reached. I'm also skeptical of anyone selling certainty right now, because the science doesn't have it yet.

Both of those can be true. That's usually where the useful answers are.

A note on worth and power

Part of what makes the psychedelic story so appealing is that it promises a shortcut. The idea that something outside you, a molecule or a guide or a ceremony, can do in an afternoon what you haven't been able to do on your own.

Sometimes these experiences really do help. But the power was never in the substance alone. It's in what you do with what you saw, and in deciding that you're worth the slower work of building a life that matches it.
