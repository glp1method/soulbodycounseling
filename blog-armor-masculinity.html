The Armor Nobody Taught You to Take Off

Emotional suppression didn't start with being gay. For a lot of us, it got reinforced twice — once by the culture, once by the community that was supposed to be safer.

Zane Guilfoyle, LPC, LAC September 2026 10 min read

A client of mine — sharp, funny, put-together in every visible way — once told me, almost as an aside, that he couldn't remember the last time he'd cried in front of another person. Not since he was a kid. He said it like it was a minor fact about himself, the way you'd mention you don't like cilantro.

It wasn't minor. It was the whole session, once we actually looked at it.

Most men are taught early to armor up — that's not a gay-specific story. But for a lot of gay and queer men, the armor gets built in a particular way, for a particular reason, and then something unexpected happens: instead of taking it off once it's safe to, a lot of us walk it straight into the one place we hoped might be different. This is about that armor. Where it comes from, why it doesn't come off on schedule, and what it actually costs to keep wearing it.

The first suit of armor

Before any of the specifically-gay stuff, there's the generic stuff — the version every boy in this culture gets handed, regardless of who he grows up to love. Don't cry. Don't need too much. Handle it yourself. Feelings are for later, or for no one.

For a kid who also senses, even before he has language for it, that something about him is different — that he's going to need to manage information carefully, read rooms other kids don't have to read, figure out who's safe before he figures out who he is — that generic armor becomes something closer to survival gear. Not "boys don't cry" as a mild cultural script. More like: if I let this show, I don't know what happens next.

So the armor goes on early, and it goes on tight, and it gets reinforced by actual evidence. It works. It keeps things stable. Nobody gets to see the parts that might not be acceptable. By the time a lot of gay men reach adulthood, the armor isn't something they're wearing anymore — it's something they've forgotten is separate from their skin.

Then you find your people. And the armor doesn't come off.

Here's the part that surprises people: you'd think coming out, finding community, building a life around other gay and queer men would be where the armor finally gets to come off. Sometimes it does. But often, something stranger happens — the community itself starts rewarding the same suppression that hurt you in the first place, just with different language.

Look at the vocabulary a lot of gay dating culture runs on: masc, straight-acting, no drama, chill, DTF, not looking for anything serious. There's a whole dialect built around signaling that you are unbothered, undemanding, low-maintenance — emotionally, if not sexually. "No fats, no femmes, no drama" isn't just about bodies. It's a filter for anyone who might actually need something from another person. Vulnerability reads, in a lot of these spaces, as a liability. Femininity reads as weakness. Wanting connection too visibly reads as needy, and needy is close to the worst thing you can be.

That's a strange inheritance. A community built, at its founding, on refusing to be shamed for who it loves ends up recycling a fairly conventional idea about what a man is allowed to feel and show. The armor that got built to survive a hostile world gets re-issued, with new packaging, by the world that was supposed to be the relief from all that.

What the armor is actually doing

I want to be careful here, because the armor isn't stupid. It's not a character flaw or a failure of self-awareness. It did its job. For a lot of my clients, that early suppression was genuinely adaptive — it kept them safe in households or towns or schools where showing the wrong feeling at the wrong time had real consequences. Armor that works gets kept. That's not pathology. That's a nervous system doing exactly what it's supposed to do.

The problem isn't that the armor was built. It's that most people never get a clear, deliberate moment to ask whether they still need it — the way you'd take off a coat once you're inside. It just stays on. It becomes the default setting, the thing that runs underneath every interaction without being examined, long after the specific threat it was built for has changed or disappeared entirely.

And armor, worn long enough, stops being something you have. It starts being something you are.

What it costs

The costs are rarely dramatic. They show up as a kind of low-grade static across a lot of areas of life.

In relationships, it looks like being the reliable one, the fun one, the one who never asks for too much — and slowly resenting everyone for not offering what you never let yourself signal you needed. It looks like ending things right around the point they'd require actual exposure.

In sex, it can look like a wall between physical intimacy and emotional intimacy that never quite comes down, even with people you've been with for years. Bodies fully available, everything else held at a careful distance.

In the body itself, it often shows up as a kind of chronic bracing — a baseline tension that's been there so long it doesn't register as tension anymore, just as how you are. Some of my clients don't discover this until we slow down enough in a session for them to actually notice their shoulders, their jaw, the low hum of readiness they've been carrying for decades.

And often, when the feeling does eventually find a way out — because it always finds a way out — it doesn't come out as sadness or need. It comes out as irritability, or shutdown, or a drink that turns into the whole bottle, or a hookup that was never really about the hookup. The feeling gets its expression. It just doesn't get named on the way there.

Taking it off is not the same as never needing it again

I'm not interested in convincing anyone the armor was a mistake, or that the goal is to walk around with no boundaries and every feeling on display all the time. That's not health. That's just a different kind of unregulated.

What I am interested in is choice. Right now, for a lot of the men I work with, the armor isn't a choice — it's a reflex that fires before any decision gets made. The work isn't about getting rid of it. It's about getting enough distance from it that you can actually decide, in a given moment, with a given person, whether it's serving you or costing you.

In session, this often starts in the body, not the story. Noticing the bracing before naming the feeling underneath it. Practicing staying present with something uncomfortable for four seconds longer than instinct wants to allow. Sometimes it means EMDR, when the armor is clearly welded to a specific memory or a specific person who taught you it was necessary. Sometimes it's slower than that — just repeated practice, in a room where nothing bad happens when the guard drops, until the nervous system starts to believe that's actually true.

It is almost never fast. The armor took years to build. It doesn't come off in one honest conversation, however good that conversation feels.

A note on worth and power

The armor was never really about hiding weakness. It was about protecting something the world had already told you wasn't safe to show — which means, underneath it, most of what's being guarded is exactly the stuff that would let you be known. Wanted. Actually met.

Real power, in this context, isn't the strength it took to build the armor — though that took real strength, and it deserves to be named as such. It's the strength it takes to occasionally set it down. On purpose. With someone who's earned it. And find out you're still standing.

That's not weakness. That's the whole point.
